Field Notes: Shipping AI Automation at a Supply Chain Startup

Six months inside Vorto.ai's product team — what we built, what broke, and what 90% AI accuracy actually means when you're the PM shipping it.

📅 March 2026 ⏱ 7 min read 🏷 Vorto.ai · AI Automation · Supply Chain · Product Ops

Vorto.ai is an AI-driven supply chain platform that matches shippers with carriers, automates dispatching, and manages the operational complexity of moving bulk materials at scale. When I joined as PM in July 2025, the core matching platform was working — but the operational layer around it was largely manual. Finance ran invoice reconciliation out of spreadsheets. Maintenance coordination happened over the phone. The result: smart infrastructure with slow, error-prone ops underneath it.

Over six months I ran three concurrent product initiatives. This is the honest account of how I approached each one — including what the 90% AI accuracy headline actually required to be useful in production.

1 — Problem Statement

Three Operational Bottlenecks Slowing a Smart Platform

Vorto's core technology was strong. The constraint on growth wasn't the matching algorithm — it was the operational overhead required to keep the network running. Three bottlenecks stood out from my initial stakeholder review:

  • Dispatching: Dispatchers used a combination of spreadsheets, phone calls, and institutional knowledge to route drivers to facilities. This worked at small network scale. At Vorto's current volume, dispatchers were spending the majority of their shift on coordination instead of exception management.
  • Invoice processing: The finance team received invoices from 20+ facilities and hundreds of vendors. Every invoice required manual categorization — by type, vendor, facility, cost category — before it could be routed for payment. At the team's invoice volume, this was 200+ analyst-hours per month in categorization alone.
  • Repair & Maintenance: Coordination across 20+ maintenance facilities was entirely paper-based. Work orders were handwritten, follow-ups happened via phone, and there was no digital visibility into maintenance status for dispatchers or fleet managers.

Each was a distinct product problem requiring a different approach. I ran them concurrently with different discovery methodologies, which is itself a lesson in product operations at startups.

The dispatching discovery surfaced the most important insight of all three projects: dispatchers didn't want automation that replaced their judgment — they wanted automation that surfaced the right options faster so they could decide better. That's a fundamentally different product. The original brief assumed we were building a replacement system. Dispatchers told us we were building a recommendation system. That distinction drove the entire product design.

The R&M discovery required a different method entirely. There were no existing users to interview in the traditional sense — the users had never experienced a digital version of their workflow. I spent two weeks shadowing maintenance coordinators at three facilities: watching physical handoffs of work orders, timing the sequence from "vehicle flagged" to "vehicle cleared," and mapping every failure point where coordination broke down. The pattern that emerged was consistent: the failures weren't in the work itself. They were in the handoffs — between shifts, between facilities and vendors, between drivers and coordinators.

For 0→1 products, discovery isn't about asking users what they want. It's about watching what breaks and counting how often.

3 — Solution & Process

Three Products, One PLM Framework

Initiative 1 · Dispatching

Automated Dispatching Recommendation Engine

Built a ranked recommendation system surfacing the top 3 dispatch options per active load, with reasoning shown. Dispatchers override with one click; overrides feed back as training signal. Designed for augmentation, not replacement — adoption was 2x faster than projected as a result.

Initiative 2 · Finance

AI Invoice Classification at 90% Accuracy

ML model classifying invoices by type, vendor, facility, and cost category. Confidence scores displayed per invoice. Invoices above threshold auto-routed; below threshold surfaced in a prioritized audit queue. Manual override flow built to be faster than the original baseline. Post-launch: added feedback loop from overrides back into model training.

Initiative 3 · R&M

0→1 Repair & Maintenance Digital Platform

Service orchestration workflow connecting drivers, facility staff, and vendors in real time. Digital work orders replacing paper. Automated follow-up notifications. Status visibility across the network without phone calls. Built as a lightweight operations system, not an AI product — the value was coordination clarity, not algorithmic intelligence.

What "90% accuracy" required in product design

90% invoice classification accuracy sounds like a win. And it is — for this use case. But it means 10% of invoices at Vorto's volume still need manual review, which was hundreds of invoices per month. The model isn't the product. The product is the model plus the review workflow that handles the exceptions effectively.

We built three things around the model that made the 90% useful: confidence score display on every classified invoice; a prioritized audit queue so reviewers saw highest-uncertainty items first; and a manual override flow that was faster than the original manual categorization process. That last piece was the key insight: if the exception path is slower than the baseline, users route everything through the exception path.

  • No classification consistency across analysts
  • No audit trail on categorization decisions
  • Finance team capacity consumed by categorization, not analysis
  • Errors discovered at reconciliation — weeks after the fact
After — AI Classification System
  • 90% of invoices auto-classified and routed without human review
  • Same-day processing for 90% of invoice volume — 5x velocity
  • Consistent classification model across all vendors and facilities
  • Full audit trail with confidence scores and override history
  • Finance capacity reallocated to analysis and exception management
  • Anomalies surfaced in real time via confidence score alerts

AI product lesson: The highest-leverage PM work isn't writing the model spec. It's the design work upstream (problem boundary, data quality standards, what "good enough" accuracy means) and downstream (human-in-the-loop for errors, feedback loops, how failures surface). Most AI product failures I've seen are product design failures, not model failures.

5 — Reflection

What I'd Do Differently

Mistake: Scoping R&M as a standalone product

The R&M platform was scoped as its own product rather than as an extension of the existing dispatching infrastructure. In retrospect, the two shared enough data (vehicle status, facility capacity, driver availability) that integrating them earlier would have created compounding value — dispatchers could see maintenance status when making routing decisions, and the R&M platform could surface real-time dispatch context to facilities. I shipped two separate systems that could have been one. This is a classic startup PM mistake: moving fast enough to ship, but not slow enough to see the integration opportunity.

Trade-off I made: PLM formalization vs. velocity

Running three concurrent initiatives at a startup with a small team required explicit PLM governance — otherwise you get coordination debt that compounds. I established a lightweight framework (discovery gate, requirement review checkpoint, post-launch review cycle) about six weeks in, after the first initiative was already mid-sprint. I should have established it before any initiative started. The six-week gap created some rework when alignment assumptions diverged between teams. At a startup, "move fast" and "establish the operating model" don't have to be in tension — but you have to make the second thing explicit from day one.

What the six months reinforced

The discipline that mattered most wasn't roadmapping or feature prioritization — it was knowing which type of product problem each initiative was. The dispatching problem was a judgment-augmentation problem (don't replace, recommend). The invoice problem was a classification problem with a required exception path. The R&M problem was a coordination problem masquerading as a tech problem. Getting the problem type right before writing a single requirement changed everything downstream.
