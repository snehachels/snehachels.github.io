Building an AI-powered Invoice Processing Automation System

How I built an end-to-end ML system that reduced invoice processing costs by 96% while maintaining 90% accuracy.

⏱ 12 min read

At a Glance

  • Role: Senior Product Manager
  • Starting State: 10K monthly invoices, >19 day cycle, $2/invoice, 12-15% error rate
  • Outcome: 78% auto-approval, 3.2 day cycle, $0.4/invoice, 90% accuracy
  • Key Skills: End-to-end system design, ML validation, LLM-based extraction, ERP integration
  • Business Impact: Financial processing increased by 5x

At Vorto, we were drowning in invoices. Every month, 10,000 supplier invoices came through in hundreds of different formats. Finance teams manually extracted data, coded them to GL accounts, routed for appropriate next steps and follow ups, queued for approval, and processed payment. The entire cycle took 18–22 days and cost $8.50 per invoice.

This was a classic problem ripe for automation. But invoice processing isn't simple. Each vendor has a different format. Line items are messy. You have to catch fraud, prevent duplicates, and maintain audit trails. I built a system that handles it all—using LLMs, OCR, validation logic, and continuous learning. The result: 78% auto-approval, 3.2-day processing, $0.32 per invoice, and $2.1M annual savings.

Here's how I did it, what went wrong, and what I'd do differently next time.

The Problem: Manual Invoice Processing at Scale

Before the project, Vorto's invoice workflow was entirely manual:

  • Finance team received invoices via email, portals, and EDI feeds
  • Manually entered vendor name, invoice number, line items, amounts
  • Matched invoices to purchase orders (when POs existed)
  • Routed to managers for approval based on amount
  • Approved invoices posted to NetSuite ERP
  • Scheduled payment

The Solution: LLM-Powered Extraction with ML Validation

I designed an end-to-end system with three key layers:

Layer 1: OCR & Text Extraction

Invoices came in as PDFs, scanned images, and email attachments—many in poor quality. The first step was converting them to machine-readable text. I used AWS Textract for OCR, but added preprocessing: image enhancement, deskewing, denoising. This got us to ~94% successful text extraction on the first try.

Layer 2: LLM-Based Data Extraction

Raw text from OCR isn't structured. I used GPT-4 with few-shot prompting to extract key fields: vendor name, invoice number, date, due date, line items, totals, and payment terms. The model returned clean JSON.

Why GPT-4 over cheaper models? I evaluated Claude (94% accuracy, cheaper) and Gemini (91% accuracy, cheapest). The 2% accuracy improvement in GPT-4 meant ~2% fewer manual reviews, worth ~$300K/year in labor savings. The API cost was $50K/year. Net benefit: $250K/year. Always measure ROI on accuracy gains.

Layer 3: Validation + Anomaly Detection

Even perfect extraction can fail if the invoice itself is problematic. I built two validation layers:

Rule-based checks: Do totals add up? Are dates reasonable? Are all required fields present? These catch obvious errors.

ML-based anomaly detection: I trained a lightweight classifier on historical data to flag suspicious invoices: duplicates (same vendor, same amount within 6 months), unknown vendors, amounts 2x above historical average, or unusual payment terms. This caught ~85% of fraudulent/duplicate invoices before approval.

Implementation: 3 Phases Over 6 Months

Phase 1: Proof of Concept (Weeks 1–6)

Goal: Show that it's feasible.

I collected 500 invoices (20 vendors, 5 formats) and built the core extraction system. Finance team manually verified results (ground truth). Accuracy: 88%. Cost per invoice: $0.02 in API calls. Speed: 1.5 seconds.

Decision: Proceed to pilot.

Phase 2: Pilot (Weeks 7–16)

Goal: Validate in production on a small set.

Processed 10K invoices (small vendors, <$5K each). Set high confidence threshold: only auto-approve if model score>0.95. Manual review queue for everything else. Monthly retraining on corrections.

Key learnings:

  • Vendor-specific performance varied wildly. Amazon invoices: 96% accuracy (consistent format). Small suppliers: 82% (inconsistent).
  • Line item extraction was the hardest part, especially with wrapped text.
  • Approval routing rules were too rigid. Added ML predictor based on historical patterns.
  • Anomaly detector had 20% false positive rate. Retrained it, dropped to 5%.

Results: Accuracy 91%, Processing time 8 days, Auto-approval rate 72%.

Phase 3: Full Rollout (Weeks 17–26)

Goal: Process all 150K monthly invoices.

Gradual rollout: 3 vendors → 12 vendors → all vendors. Confidence thresholds evolved: new vendors (>0.98), known vendors (>0.92).

Technical Decisions & Tradeoffs

Decision 1: Confidence Thresholds

I could have set thresholds at 0.75 (90% auto-approve, 5% error) or 0.98 (conservative, 40% auto-approve, 0.1% error). I chose 0.90 (balanced).

The math: Cost of one false approval ~$500 (rework + compliance risk). Cost of one manual review = $12.50. Break-even point: error rate < 2.5%. At 0.90 threshold, I achieved 1.2% error rate. Worth it.

Decision 2: Retraining Frequency

Monthly retraining on corrections was the sweet spot. Weekly was noisy, quarterly was slow. Each month we captured ~5K corrected invoices, retrained, A/B tested new model vs. old, and deployed if >2% improvement. First 4 months: 1-2% monthly improvement. Then plateau at 96%.

Decision 3: In-House vs. Vendor Solution

I could have bought an off-the-shelf AP automation tool. But they cost $500K+/year and lack customization. I built this for $370K and owned the IP. For a company at Vorto's scale, building was cheaper and better.

Quality Evaluation: How I Measured Success

The hardest part wasn't building the system—it was knowing if it was working.

Building Ground Truth

I built a manually-verified dataset of 1,000 invoices where the finance team extracted "correct" data. Blind to model predictions. This became my test set for all accuracy measurements.

Failure Mode Analysis

Why did 8% of extractions fail? I categorized them:

  • OCR failure (2%): Blurry images, non-English text. Solution: Route to manual review.
  • LLM confusion (3%): Non-standard layout, merged columns. Solution: Manual review + feedback for retraining.
  • Business logic (2%): Wrong GL codes, wrong approver. Solution: Better rules + ML training.
  • Data quality (1%): Incomplete invoice. Solution: Alert vendor.

What I'd Do Differently

Start with Smaller Scope

I tried to handle all 150K invoices from day one. Better approach: master 10 high-volume vendors (80K invoices) first. Then expand incrementally. This would have avoided overengineering.

Involve Finance Team Earlier

I built the system, then showed it to finance. They had requirements I missed: audit trails, export for audits, vendor-specific performance tracking. Co-design from week 1 is better.

Plan for New Vendors

When a new vendor joined, accuracy dropped to 75%. Better approach: template system for onboarding. Collect 20 sample invoices, quick retraining, phased rollout.

Build Explainability

The system was a black box: "92% accurate." Finance team didn't trust it. Better approach: explain each extraction. "Vendor name = Amazon (confidence 0.98 based on logo + formatting)." This doubled user confidence.

Key Lessons

1. Accuracy Isn't Everything

A 92% accurate system that catches fraud is better than a 98% accurate system that misses it. Measure what matters: false negatives (errors that slip through), false positives (false alarms), and business impact (money saved).

2. Evaluation is Hard

Building ground truth is expensive and tedious. But it's the only way to know if you're improving. Invest early in measurement infrastructure.

3. Feedback Loops Drive Improvement

Monthly retraining on corrections led to 1-2% monthly improvement. Trying to get a "perfect" model upfront would have failed. Ship fast, iterate, learn.

What This Work Prepared Me For

End-to-End System Thinking

  • This wasn't just a machine learning problem. It was OCR + LLM + validation logic + approval workflow + ERP integration. I learned to think about systems holistically: where does data come from, how does it flow, what checks do we add, what gets prioritized. This translates to any complex automation or data pipeline project.

Measuring What Matters

  • It's easy to optimize for model accuracy. Harder to optimize for business impact. I learned to measure: false negatives (errors that slip through), false positives (false alarms), and business value. This discipline transfers to any AI/ML project.

Stakeholder Trust in AI

  • Finance teams don't adopt AI systems if they don't trust them. I learned that accuracy metrics alone don't build trust. Explainability does. Transparency does. Involving stakeholders in evaluation does. This is critical for any AI rollout in a regulated or high-stakes environment.

Iterative Improvement

  • We didn't ship a perfect system in month 1. We shipped a 88% accurate PoC, learned monthly on 5K corrections, and improved to 92% in production. The discipline of monthly feedback loops and A/B testing is something I apply to every project now.

Financial Rigor in Engineering Decisions

  • Every technical choice had a business case. Which LLM model? Calculate ROI. What confidence threshold? Do the math. What retraining frequency? Measure impact. This financial discipline separates good engineers from great ones.

The Numbers That Matter

When evaluating AI projects, ask:

  • Before/After: Was 19 days → 3.2 days. Was $8.50/invoice → $0.32/invoice. Was 12-15% error → 1.2% error.
  • Trade-offs: 92% accuracy (1.2% false negative), 78% auto-approval (22% still need human), $370K/year cost, 7.8x ROI.
  • Risk: If accuracy drops to 85%, auto-approval drops to 55%. Acceptable. If fraud slips through, loss ~$100K. Caught by audits.
  • Scaling: Handles 150K invoices now. Tested to 300K. New vendors: 24-hour onboarding. Works.

Conclusion

Invoice processing isn't glamorous. But it's high-volume, error-prone, and expensive. Perfect for AI. I built a system that extracts structured data from messy documents, validates it, routes it intelligently, and learns from every correction. The result is faster, cheaper, more accurate processing—and it freed up finance team to focus on strategy instead of data entry.

If you're considering an AI automation project, here's the playbook: understand the business case first. Start with a pilot. Measure rigorously. Iterate monthly. Involve stakeholders early. And always remember—good enough AI that ships is better than perfect AI that never launches.
