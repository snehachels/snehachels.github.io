I Built a PRD Generator with LLMs — Here's What It Actually Does Well

A Kellogg side project turned into my most-used PM tool. The honest story of what works, what doesn't, and what building it revealed about writing better requirements.

⏱ 7 min read

By the time a PM sits down to write a PRD, the hard intellectual work is largely done. You've done the discovery, you understand the problem, you know what needs to be built and why. The frustrating part is the translation: taking 4–6 weeks of accumulated context and turning it into a structured, 8-page document that a diverse set of stakeholders can act on. That translation takes me 4–6 hours per PRD when done properly.

In fall 2023, I was taking a course at Kellogg called Data Analytics with LLMs and simultaneously staring down a backlog of product briefs for a B2B SaaS project I was working on. I had a specific problem and a new set of tools. I thought: what if I could use an LLM to generate the structured first draft, and focus my own energy on reviewing, refining, and filling gaps rather than generating?

Four months of iteration produced something I still use. Here's the honest account.

1 — Problem Statement

The Real Cost of PRD Writing for PMs

PRDs are one of the highest-leverage things a PM produces — a good PRD aligns engineering, design, and business stakeholders on a single source of truth before a line of code is written. A bad PRD causes rework, misaligned builds, and scope creep that costs weeks.

The cost of writing them well is time that could go to discovery, stakeholder conversations, or post-launch analysis. In a consulting engagement context (where I was during the Kellogg project), that trade-off was especially acute: discovery cycles were short, stakeholder expectations were high, and a PRD backlog was a real constraint on team velocity.

I wanted to reduce the time cost of the translation layer — not the thinking layer. The goal wasn't to use AI to replace product judgment. It was to use AI to do the structural, mechanical parts of PRD assembly so my time could go to the judgment-intensive parts.

2 — Research & Insights

Validating the Problem Before Building the Solution

Before writing a single prompt, I ran a small informal study. I asked five other PMs in my Kellogg cohort to time-box a PRD writing session and note where their time actually went. The pattern was consistent across all five:

  • Problem statement and executive summary: 20–30 minutes (high cognitive load, but fast once you start)
  • User research section: 45–60 minutes (required pulling from interview notes and synthesizing)
  • Acceptance criteria: 60–90 minutes (tedious translation from feature description to testable criterion)
  • Out of scope section: 30–45 minutes (requires anticipating what engineers might assume is in scope)
  • Success metrics: 20–30 minutes (straightforward once the problem statement is sharp)

The acceptance criteria and user research synthesis were the biggest time sinks — and both were tasks where an LLM with the right context might perform well. That shaped the build priorities.

3 — Solution & Process

Three Versions, Three Different Architectures

Version 1 · Week 1

Naive Generation — "Just Write Me a PRD"

Single prompt, structured intake form (15 questions), one-shot output. Structure was correct, content was generic and fabricated. The model wrote "users will accomplish their goals more efficiently" — technically true, useless in a PRD. Root cause: asking the model to generate content it didn't have.

Version 2 · Weeks 2–4

Context Injection — "Edit This Raw Material"

Stop treating the LLM as a brain; treat it as an editor. Feed in raw context — interview notes, Slack threads, data findings, competitive research — and ask it to synthesize and structure. Output quality jumped significantly. Key discovery: the model was good at pulling quotes from interview notes and placing them in the right section.

Version 3 · Weeks 5–16

Section-by-Section Chaining

One-shot full PRD generation produced lower quality than prompting section by section. Each section needed a different prompting strategy. Built an 8-prompt chain where each section had its own context window, instructions, and output format. Prior sections passed context forward. Quality stabilized at "good first draft" for 90% of sections.

The architecture insight that mattered most: treating prompt engineering like product design. Each prompt was a mini-spec. The output was the user test. The iteration cycle was fast. The question for each prompt was: what is the reader of this section trying to decide, and what information do they need to make that decision well?

# Example: Acceptance Criteria prompt (section 6 of 8)
CONTEXT: Feature description from section 5: {feature_desc}
  Technical constraints from PRD header: {constraints}
  Out-of-scope items from section 4: {out_of_scope}

TASK: Generate acceptance criteria for this feature.
  Each criterion must be:
  - Testable by a QA engineer without product context
  - Written as "Given [state] when [action] then [outcome]"
  - Specific enough to catch common edge cases
  - Consistent with the out-of-scope items listed above

FORMAT: Numbered list. Max 8 criteria. Flag any
  ambiguity in the feature description as [NEEDS CLARIFICATION].
4 — Impact & Metrics

Before vs. After: PRD Output Quality

Before — Writing PRDs Manually
  • 4–6 hours per PRD, mostly in structural assembly
  • Acceptance criteria written last when cognitive energy was lowest
  • Out-of-scope section often vague or incomplete under time pressure
  • User research quotes buried in notes, not surfaced into document
  • Inconsistent format across PRDs in the same product area
  • First draft required significant revision before stakeholder review
After — LLM-Assisted PRD
  • 90 min to structured first draft; remaining time on judgment-intensive edits
  • Acceptance criteria generated in ~10 min from feature description
  • Out-of-scope section expanded by model — flags assumptions engineers often make
  • Interview quotes auto-surfaced from notes into correct context sections
  • Consistent structure across all PRDs in the chain
  • First draft typically requires targeted edits, not structural rewrites

What the tool does well vs. where it falls short

Works well

  • First draft of acceptance criteria (~45 min saved per PRD)
  • Extracting and placing user research quotes from raw notes
  • "Out of scope" section — anticipates unspoken engineer assumptions
  • Success metrics first draft from a clear problem statement
  • Identifying contradictions in stakeholder notes when both are provided
  • Competitive comparison from raw research inputs

Doesn't work well

  • Institutional knowledge it doesn't have (tech debt, team capacity)
  • Nuanced prioritization — produces a list but not ranked by your context
  • Novel product thinking — synthesizes well, doesn't generate insights
  • Knowing when a requirement is infeasible for your specific stack
  • Generating the "why" — it structures the what, not the reasoning
5 — Reflection

The Unexpected Lesson

The most valuable thing I got out of building this tool wasn't the time savings. It was what building it forced me to understand about PRDs.

To write prompts that produced useful output, I had to be explicit about what each section of a PRD actually needs to communicate — not abstractly, but specifically: what is the reader of this section trying to decide, and what information do they need to make that decision well? Asking that question about the "acceptance criteria" section forced me to understand that its real reader isn't the designer — it's the QA engineer. Asking it about "out of scope" forced me to understand that its real reader is the engineer who will otherwise make reasonable assumptions that turn into wrong builds.

That clarity made me a better PRD author even when I wasn't using the tool. The discipline of writing a structured intake form that the LLM could act on also improved my discovery process — the intake form was a checklist of questions a good PRD should answer, which meant any gap in the form was a gap in my discovery.

The tool is a mirror. It shows you what your requirements actually say, stripped of the prose that makes them sound more complete than they are. That's useful even when the output is bad — especially when the output is bad.

What I'd build differently

The current tool is a Python script and a set of text files. It's not a product — it's a workflow. I deliberately didn't try to generalize it beyond my own use case, which kept the build focused and the iteration fast. The right scope for a side project is the scope that solves the problem you actually have.

If I were to productize it, the first thing I'd add is a structured validation step: before generating any section, the model checks whether the context provided is sufficient to generate that section at acceptable quality. Right now, insufficient context produces confident-sounding bad output. A validation gate would surface the gaps before they compound into a document full of plausible-but-wrong content.

This project was built during my MBA at the Kellogg School of Management (2023–2024), drawing on the course Data Analytics with LLMs and concurrent product strategy work. The approach has continued to evolve since then. The code is not public.
