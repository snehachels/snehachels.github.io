Building Mercedes-Benz's AI Solutions Division

From just an idea with no roadmap to 70% adoption across the org

⏱ 8 min read

In 2017, I joined Mercedes-Benz's Bengaluru engineering center as a program manager responsible for fixing engineering quality issues across multiple carlines. However, I noticed that the engineer workflows were pretty constrained: manual trial-and-error testing of design points, manual research, and long simulation cycles. The bottleneck was obvious - we were running hundreds of simulations to isolate which design combinations might reproduce a given issue, when our own historical measurement and testing data could have predicted the likely candidates.

This encouraged me to learn about data manipulation, ML techniques and applications in engineering usecases. I started building out business cases and PoCs for different problem statements and slowly built a team of AI enthusiasts interested in being part of this initiative.

Three years later, the AI Solutions division had 7+ ML-based analytics products in production, 70% of the organization actively using the app relevant to them, and $5M/year in documented operational efficiencies.

1 - Problem Statement

The Business Problem: Decisions Made Without Data

The Bengaluru engineering center operated across warranty, procurement, manufacturing quality, and after-sales. Each function was making high-stakes decisions using domain expertise of the research engineers and their capability of reaching the solution through tril-and-error.

The operational cost was significant:

  • Delayed escalations: 3–4 days to surface a quality issue from raw data
  • Engineering overhead: 27% of senior engineering capacity consumed by manual data prep and running simulations
  • No proactivity: quality issues caught reactively after escalation, not before

The data existed. What didn't exist was the intelligence layer to turn it into fast, consistent and proactive insights.


2 - Research & Discovery

Five Months of Structured Discovery

My first decision as a first-time PM was to run structured discovery across the organization to find where AI could provide clear, attributable value.

For each research lead, I asked: What's the most important decision you make weekly? What information do you need? How long does it take to get? What happens when you make it on incomplete data?

And for each function, I asked: What's the most important metric you need to deliver? What are your requirements and constraints for a new initiative?

The insight: Most of the extended simulation time based problems could be fixed with calibrated ML models customized to the research probelm they were working on. A simple solution - but the before state was so inefficient that the modest ML improvement would create disproportinate value.


3 - Solution & Process

Building the Division in Phases

Phase 1 · Months 1–3

User Workflow Research and Prioritization

Identified high-frequency, high-impact workflows across the org. Pioritized the build order and devised a roadmap. Shadowed the workflows to identify edge cases and constraints. Accessed and built the data pipelines and tested data manipulatio pipelines. Researched various tech providers and established a tech vendor relationship on a trial basis.

Phase 2 · Months 3–6

MVP and Validation

Built clustering and analomy detection based models for various workflows to validate the hypothesis that ML could move the needle on business outcomes. Presented results to executives and in townhall meetings to build a champions team to push the work forward.

Phase 3 · Months 6–12

Pilot Launch + User Research Loop & Iteration

Launched the first two products to a few engineers. Weekly feedback cycles. Discovered users wanted less complexity, not more features and tools to learn. Reduced core workflow to just a few clicks. Result: WAU +300%, NPS +35 within 2 months.

Phase 4 · Years 2–3

Platform Expansion: 7+ Products

Scaled to different research and post-sales teams. Coached multiple engineers to experiment with new builds, gather user feedback and iterate on new usecases.

Phase 5 · Year 3

Low-Code Platform Migration

Adopted Dataiku (a low-code automation platform) as the standardized platform all AI solutions in the company. This strategic shift meant more engineers could build and scale products.

Phase 6 · Years 4–5

Scale & Adoption Operations

Established division-wide PLM governance: standardized PRD template, quarterly roadmap reviews, post-launch NPS tracking. Ensured quality checks of results were in place.

Stakeholder Ecosystem

Different stakeholders had different constraints:

  • Engineering cared about technical feasibility and team capacity
  • IT wanted centraliation and least amount of chaos
  • Finance cared about ROI and resource allocation
  • Operations cared about user readiness and change management
  • Quality cared about reliability and edge cases

    • Two questions always helped me align the stakeholders: What would change your mind? What adoption threshold would trigger a shift? That conversation was worth more than the decision itself. It meant stakeholders weren't just executing roadmap - they were thinking alongside it. After all, diverse perspectives that can speak freely make better decisions. Additionally, communicating the vision and the final benefit in metrics that were relevant to that particular stakeholder and listing risks transparently brought them onboard more easily.

    • Engineers shifted from "executing analysis" to "owning strategy". That work had been managed from overseas, handled by a distributed team without local context.     • New employees onboarded differently, too. Instead of learning through institutional knowledge - "here's how we've always done this" - they could see decision-making logic in dashboards. They understood not just what was decided, but why. That compressed onboarding from months to weeks.

    5 - Reflection

    What This Work Prepared Me For

    Building under ambiguity

    No precedent existed. No playbook, no prior art internally. I learned to make decisions with 70% information instead of waiting for 100% certainty. This translates directly to early-stage products, venture environments, and emerging technology spaces where the path isn't known yet. Comfort with ambiguity isn't just a nice-to-have, it's the core competency.

    Operating in Matrix Organizations

    I coordinated 55+ stakeholders across competing priorities - engineering, finance, IT, operations. I learned that in large organizations, systems beat relationships at scale. A PM can't rely on personal credibility alone; you need formal governance, decision-making structures, and escalation paths. This prepares me for senior roles where influence comes through architecture, not persuasion.

    Scaling Products Post-PMF

    Building one product is fundamentally different from scaling seven. At Mercedes, I learned that speed in scaling comes from friction removal, not feature addition. We achieved 300% WAU growth by simplifying workflows, not by adding intelligence. This changes how I think about product roadmaps, engineering investment, and where optimization actually pays off.

    Product Leadership in Regulated & Complex Environments

    Mercedes is a regulated, risk-conscious organization. I learned to design systems with constraints, not despite them. Data governance, compliance, stakeholder approval - these aren't obstacles to work around. They're requirements that, when designed for early, make the product stronger. This directly applies to healthcare, finance, supply chain, and other high-stakes domains.

    Responsible AI & Trustworthy Systems

    I learned that adoption of AI systems requires ethical clarity from day one. Human-in-loop design, transparency, user control—these aren't afterthoughts. They're product design decisions that determine whether people trust the system. As AI becomes mainstream, this distinction between "technically correct" and "trustworthy" becomes the core differentiator.

    What I'd Do Differently

    Mistake: Delayed formalization of governance

    For two years, stakeholder buy-in was informal - good relationships, monthly emails. However, when it was time to scale through the org and engage multiple engineers in the division, governance based credibility was missing. Built a formalized PLM governance for the division which helped immensely in enhancing the products.

    Trade-off: Speed over data quality standards

    Two products underperformed in production not because the ML model was wrong, but because the underlying SAP data had consistency issues we hadn't audited before scoping. We prioritized shipping speed over rigorous data quality review. Today I build data quality assessment into discovery as a first-class requirement, not an engineering task to handle later.

    The bigger lesson

    Building an entrepreneurial division inside a large company is itself a product problem. The product isn't just the software — it's the operating model: who reviews the roadmap, how data quality gets owned, what happens when a model degrades, how new use cases get discovered and prioritized. This experience helped me become good at designing systems.
