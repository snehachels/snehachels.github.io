Building Mercedes-Benz's AI Solutions Division from Scratch

From a mandate with no roadmap to 400% adoption across 70% of the org — what the five-year build actually looked like from the inside.

⏱ 8 min read

In 2017, I joined Mercedes-Benz in Bengaluru as an engineer who had decided she wanted to build products. The mandate I was handed was simultaneously exciting and paralyzing: "leverage AI and data to drive better decisions across the organization." No team. No budget template. No product map. No prior art internally to copy from.

Five years later, the AI Solutions division had 7+ ML-based analytics products in production, 70% of the organization actively using at least one of them, and $5M/year in documented operational efficiencies. This is how it happened.

1 — Problem Statement

The Business Problem: Decisions Made Without Data

The Bengaluru engineering center operated across warranty, procurement, manufacturing quality, and after-sales. Each function was making high-stakes decisions using SAP exports, Excel pivot tables, and institutional memory.

The operational cost was significant:

  • Delayed escalations: 3–4 days to surface a quality issue from raw data
  • Inconsistent analysis: each analyst structured Excel models differently, leading to conflicting conclusions
  • Engineering overhead: 27% of senior engineering capacity consumed by manual data prep ($1.8M/year)
  • No proactivity: quality issues caught reactively after escalation, not before

The data existed. What didn't exist was the intelligence layer to turn it into fast, consistent insight.

2 — Research & Discovery

Three Months of Structured Discovery

My first decision as a first-time PM was to not write requirements for 90 days. Instead, I ran structured discovery across the organization to find where AI could provide clear, attributable value.

For each function, I asked: What's the most important decision you make weekly? What information do you need? How long does it take to get? What happens when you make it on incomplete data?

The insight: the highest-value use cases weren't the most technically interesting ones. Warranty analytics was unglamorous — but the before-state was so bad that a modest ML improvement would create disproportionate value.

The best first product in a division-building context is one where the before-and-after story tells itself in a single demo. Flashy AI is easy to build. Attributable impact is hard to argue with.

3 — Solution & Process

Building the Division in Phases

Phase 1 · Months 1–6

MVP: Warranty Analytics

Anomaly detection in warranty claims, surfacing unusual failure patterns. Proof point that AI could move the needle on real business problems.

Phase 2 · Months 6–18

User Research Loop & Iteration

Monthly feedback cycles. Discovered users wanted less complexity, not more features. Reduced core workflow to 3 clicks. Result: WAU +300%, NPS +35 within 2 months.

Phase 3 · Years 2–3

Platform Expansion: 7+ Products

Scaled to procurement, quality, after-sales. Each PRD went through 55+ stakeholder review cycles. Learned that building a division is itself a product problem: the operating model matters as much as the software.

Phase 4 · Year 3

Low-Code Platform Migration

Adopted Dataiku to reduce marginal engineering cost. This strategic shift meant we could scale products without proportionally scaling headcount.

Phase 5 · Years 4–5

Scale & Adoption Operations

Established division-wide PLM governance: standardized PRD template, quarterly roadmap reviews, post-launch NPS tracking. Formalization was critical — informal relationships couldn't survive leadership changes.

The Prioritization Decision That Mattered Most

Year two: breadth (new business units) vs. depth (stronger existing products). Stakeholders wanted breadth. I chose depth — investing in a 50-interview discovery sprint that surfaced that users were abandoning workflows due to UX friction. Fixing that yielded +300% WAU and +35 NPS before we shipped a single new product. This earned the trust to move into breadth.

5 — Reflection

What I'd Do Differently

Mistake: Delayed formalization of governance

For two years, stakeholder buy-in was informal — good relationships, monthly emails. That worked until a leadership change in a major business unit. The new leader had no context, no review history, no reason to prioritize our products. We lost three months rebuilding credibility. I should have formalized the quarterly review process in year one.

Trade-off: Speed over data quality standards

Two products underperformed in production not because the ML model was wrong, but because the underlying SAP data had consistency issues we hadn't audited before scoping. We prioritized shipping speed over rigorous data quality review. Today I build data quality assessment into discovery as a first-class requirement, not an engineering task to handle later.

The bigger lesson

Building a division inside a large company is itself a product problem. The product isn't just the software — it's the operating model: who reviews the roadmap, how data quality gets owned, what happens when a model degrades, how new use cases get discovered and prioritized. The PM who thrives here isn't just good at writing PRDs. They're good at designing systems.

This case study covers my work as Product Manager, AI and Data Product Development at Mercedes-Benz (2017–2022), where I founded and led the AI Solutions division in Bengaluru, India. All metrics are from internal reporting. Views are my own.
