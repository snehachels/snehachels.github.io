Governing AI at Scale: Supply Chain Automation QA & Governance

Post-launch governance frameworks for AI automation in dispatching and R&M. How to catch failures before they cascade, maintain quality as systems scale, and build feedback loops that improve over time.

⏱ 8 min read

Most AI product conversations end at launch. The harder problem starts after: how do you ensure an automated system that worked in testing continues to work when faced with millions of real-world variations? At Vorto, I shipped three concurrent AI initiatives — dispatching recommendations, invoice classification, R&M coordination. The launch got the headlines. The governance work determined whether those systems actually survived in production.

This is the story of building quality assurance and governance frameworks that transformed AI systems from impressive demos into reliable, continuously-improving operational infrastructure.

1 — The Governance Problem

Why AI Systems Fail in Production (And How to Prevent It)

The moment an AI system goes live, three things happen immediately: user behavior changes, edge cases emerge, and model performance begins to drift. Dispatchers learned to override recommendations in ways we didn't predict. Invoices came in formats the training data never covered. Maintenance requests escalated when the R&M system missed a pattern. None of this is visible in launch metrics.

Without explicit governance, you're flying blind. You ship a 90% accurate model. Three months later it's still showing 90% accuracy in your eval metrics — but you're missing entire failure categories that only surface when users interact with the system at scale.

The product isn't the model. The product is the model plus the governance framework that keeps it honest in production.

2 — Value to Stakeholders

Who Benefits From Governance, and How

Dispatchers: Visibility into why recommendations are being made (explainability), confidence scores on each suggestion, and a feedback loop where their overrides improve tomorrow's model. Without this: system feels like a black box, adoption stalls, trust erodes.

Finance Team: Real-time audit trails on invoice classifications, confidence thresholds that flag uncertain decisions, and monthly reconciliation reports. Without this: you inherit the old problem you were trying to solve — manual verification overhead just moved to a different process.

Operations Leaders: Early detection of system failures, root cause analysis, and predictive alerts before quality degrades. Without this: you discover problems the hard way — through user complaints or business metrics dropping unexpectedly.

Product Team: Structured feedback on where the system is struggling, which edge cases matter most, and which refinements will have the highest impact. Without this: you're guessing about what to improve next.

3 — Governance Framework

From Foundational QA to Sophisticated Monitoring

Phase 1: Foundational Quality Assurance

What it covers: Core system stability, major failure modes, output consistency across high-volume standard scenarios.

  • Dispatching: 100% coverage of recommendations flagged for review. Confidence score accuracy (>95% precision on high-confidence recommendations). Override rate tracking (identify patterns in what dispatchers reject).
  • Invoice Processing: 100% coverage of auto-classified invoices. Accuracy validation on high-volume invoice types. Audit queue prioritization (surfacing lowest-confidence classifications first).
  • R&M Coordination: System uptime and latency monitoring (SLA >99%). Data completeness checks across all facility inputs. Alert routing accuracy (critical work orders reaching the right people).

Success criteria: Precision >95%, Recall >85%, All critical failures detected within 24 hours of occurrence.

Phase 2: Sophisticated Monitoring & Feedback Loops

What it covers: Model performance under distribution shift, feedback loop integrity, exception handling.

  • Dispatching: Model drift detection (recommendation accuracy degradation over time). Override feedback loop validation (corrections being fed back into retraining). Cohort analysis (performance across different dispatcher skill levels, facility types, or time of day).
  • Invoice Processing: Classification accuracy by vendor, by invoice type, by facility. Audit queue effectiveness (are we catching the actual errors?). Manual override patterns (what types of invoices are humans correcting?).
  • R&M Coordination: Task completion latency (time from work order creation to completion). Resolution quality (are work orders actually resolved, or re-opened?).

Success criteria: >75% accuracy on edge cases. Model retraining triggered within 5% accuracy degradation. Exception handling path accuracy >90%.

Out-of-Scope for Automation

Clear boundaries matter. These decisions require human judgment:

  • Safety-critical failures: Dispatcher receives recommendation that could compromise driver safety — routed to safety team regardless of system confidence.
  • Policy exceptions: Invoice classification where policy is ambiguous or disputed — routed to finance leadership, not auto-classified.
  • Vendor disputes: R&M work order where vendor claims the work wasn't completed correctly — requires investigation, not automated resolution.
  • Systemic issues: When >5% of a cohort fails in the same way — signals a model gap that needs retraining, not just exception handling.
4 — Metrics & Monitoring

What Gets Measured, What Gets Managed

Performance Metrics (How well is the system working?)

  • Dispatching: Recommendation precision, override rate by dispatcher, average time-to-decision with vs. without recommendation.
  • Invoice: Classification accuracy by type, false positive rate (incorrectly classified), manual review rate (% invoices requiring audit queue review).
  • R&M: Task completion SLA, first-contact resolution rate, re-open rate (rework).

Adoption Metrics (Is the system being used?)

  • % of eligible items receiving automated processing (dispatchers acting on recommendations, invoices auto-routed, work orders created digitally).
  • Average sessions/user/week for systems with interactive dashboards.
  • "Insight-to-Action" conversion rate (feedback provided in dashboards → system improvements implemented).

Impact Metrics (Is it better than before?)

  • Time-to-insight (dispatch decisions, invoice processing, work order assignment).
  • Error rate reduction (vs. manual baseline where applicable).
  • Cost per transaction (cost of processing a dispatch decision, an invoice, a work order).
  • User satisfaction (NPS on system feedback, adoption variance across different teams).

Most important metric: How long does it take to detect degradation? Ideally <24 hours. If you're discovering problems weeks after they started, your governance framework isn't doing its job.

5 — Risks & Mitigations

What Can Go Wrong (And How to Prepare)

Model Degradation at Scale

Risk: System works on pilot data but fails when exposed to production volume, new edge cases, or demographic shifts in the data.

Mitigation: Cohort-level monitoring by facility, dispatcher experience level, invoice type. Automated alerts when any cohort performance drops >5%. Model retraining cadence (weekly evaluation, monthly retraining if needed).

User Distrust From Over-Automation

Risk: Dispatchers feel their judgment is being replaced rather than augmented. Finance team sees audit queue as overhead instead of safety net. Adoption stalls.

Mitigation: Explicit positioning of systems as augmentation, not replacement. Transparent confidence scores (show users when the system is uncertain). Feedback dashboards framed as "opportunities to improve" not "errors." Regular communication of system improvements driven by user feedback.

Feedback Loop Failures

Risk: Corrections aren't being captured, retraining doesn't happen on schedule, the system becomes outdated while you think it's being improved.

Mitigation: Explicit data collection on every user override/correction. Audit the feedback loop itself (are we actually training on new data?). Test model versions against holdout test sets before pushing to production.

Integration & Technical Debt

Risk: Governance data doesn't flow cleanly into existing systems. Monitoring requires manual intervention. Operational burden of the QA system becomes higher than the value it provides.

Mitigation: Design governance infrastructure before launch (don't bolt it on after). Automation all monitoring and alerting. Regular technical debt reviews to catch accumulating complexity.

6 — Operational Rhythms

How Governance Actually Happens Day-to-Day

Daily Standup

30 minutes. Review automated alerts from overnight monitoring. Classify issues: expected behavior vs. anomaly. Route anomalies to appropriate team.

Weekly Quality Review

1 hour. Performance metrics across all systems. Cohort-level analysis (how is the model performing for dispatcher X vs. dispatcher Y?). Decision: proceed as-is, or flag for investigation.

Monthly Governance Review

2 hours. Cross-functional (product, operations, engineering). Review adoption metrics, impact metrics, feedback from users. Prioritize improvements: model retraining, feature additions, threshold adjustments. Plan next month's focus areas.

Quarterly Tech Partnership Review

With your ML/AI vendor (if applicable): performance SLAs, cost, roadmap alignment. Discuss model refresh strategy, new features, technical limitations.

  • No clear escalation path for anomalies
  • Retraining happens ad-hoc if at all

With Governance Framework
  • Daily monitoring from day one
  • Issues detected within 24 hours automatically
  • Every override tracked and fed back into model
  • Performance measured across 15+ cohorts continuously
  • Clear ownership for each alert type
  • Scheduled retraining based on drift signals
  • Users see system improving based on their feedback
